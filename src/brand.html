01 — What the research turned up

Four things worth building on

Pulled from their website, their newsletter, and their Facebook. Every quote below is theirs, not written for them.

The line they buried

“A welcoming community of seekers, believers, and doubters.”

That sentence is on their Facebook bio. Their actual homepage opens with UCC boilerplate used by thousands of congregations. Doubters is the word no church puts on its front door — and the one that gets a hesitant 28-year-old through it. It becomes the headline.

The joy is already there

“Another 90s song that begs to be played on a church!”

They post videos of pop songs played on the 1893 Hook & Hastings tracker organ — an instrument an organ company called the finest of its kind in New England. A landmark treated with affection instead of reverence. That is the brand’s sense of humour, and it is free.

The building is the logo

No mascot needed. A clock tower that has chimed over Taylor Park since 1893 is already a civic landmark that every person in St. Albans can picture with their eyes closed.

It changes with the seasons, which gives the site a living, recurring visual system at zero production cost.

The brave thing

Open & Affirming since 16 May 2021 — explicitly welcoming across sexual orientation and gender identity. In Franklin County, Vermont, that is a genuine differentiator, not a checkbox.

Their own covenant says it best: “We have no rigid formulation of doctrine. Our overarching creed is love.”

04 — Typography

Fraunces & Figtree

Fraunces carries the voice of the church. It is an old-style serif with a slight wobble in its curves — warm and a little imperfect, like a building that has settled for 133 years. It never appears below 19 px.

Figtree handles everything a person has to use: navigation, buttons, service times, forms. Rounded terminals, generous x-height, completely unintimidating at 15 px on a phone in a car park.

Both are open-licence and embedded directly, so nothing depends on an external font service.

05 — The structural device

Everything sits under an arch

The building is Richardsonian Romanesque, and its defining feature is the round arch — in the windows, the porte-cochère, the doorways, the tower louvres.

So the site’s containers are not rounded rectangles. Photographs, feature cards, and section openers are capped with a true semicircle. It gives the warmth and generosity that made the Duolingo reference appealing, but it is drawn from this church’s own masonry rather than borrowed from an app.

One CSS rule, used everywhere: border-radius: 9999px 9999px 0 0

06 — Components

Warm, obvious, unintimidating

This is where the Duolingo influence actually lives: one clear action per screen, buttons with real physical depth, and language that assumes you know nothing and judges you for none of it.

Buttons

Press-down depth on the primary button — the one borrowed mechanic worth keeping. It makes an action feel physical and safe rather than committal.

First-visit card

What actually happens

The service runs an hour. Wear whatever you own — jeans are normal. Park behind the church or on Church Street. Nobody will ask you to stand up, introduce yourself, or give money.

Children are welcome in the service, and there is a nursery if you would rather.

07 — The idea worth stealing

Choose your pew

Free, no obligation, takes four seconds. It solves the single most common anxiety a first-time visitor has — where do I sit and will I be in someone’s seat? — and it quietly tells the church how many chairs and how much coffee to expect. Try it.

Chancel · Organ & Choir

Pick any pew. Nobody is watching, and you can change your mind on Sunday.

Built from the real room: curved pews either side of a centre aisle, seating roughly 350. Balcony rows sit behind. On the live site this would post to a simple list the administrator can read — no login, no account, just a first name.

10 — Voice

How it should sound

Do

  • “Seekers, believers, and doubters.”
  • “Wear whatever you own.”
  • “The service runs an hour. Coffee afterwards.”
  • “You do not have to believe anything yet.”
  • “Our overarching creed is love.” (theirs)
  • “Yes, we played that on the organ.”

Don’t

  • “Come as you are” — worn out, means nothing now.
  • “Doing life together” and similar church-marketing dialect.
  • “Experience the presence” — promises a feeling nobody can guarantee.
  • Exclamation marks stacked three deep.
  • Stock photography of strangers with their hands raised.
  • Anything a 74-year-old member would find embarrassing.

The test for every line: would it make sense read aloud at coffee hour, and would it reassure someone standing outside on the pavement deciding whether to come in?

11 — Before we design a single page

Broken things found on the current site

The <title> tag is empty. Every browser tab and every Google result is blank. This is the single highest-value fix on the list.

Weekly bulletins are .docx.pdf embeds. Their most regular content is effectively unreadable on a phone. It should be a web page.

Two different music directors. The staff page says Bailey Brown; the music page says to contact Erin Grainger. One of them is out of date.

The sanctuary seats 350 — or 380. Both numbers appear, on three different pages.

“This slideshow requires JavaScript.” Printed on the Building Use page, where the wedding photographs should be.

The history page is four PDF links. The clock, the windows and the organ are the most interesting things they own.
